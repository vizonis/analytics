Complete Reference

Every Power BI Chart Explained

From the simplest line chart to advanced matrix visuals — here's your complete guide with real-world examples.

Trend Analysis

1. Line Chart

A line chart connects data points with a continuous line to show trends over time. It's the most fundamental time-series visual in Power BI. Best for showing how a metric changes across a continuous axis like dates, months, or years.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Tracking a metric over time (continuous data)
  • • Comparing trends of 2-3 categories
  • • Showing rate of change (steepness = speed)

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Monthly website traffic over 12 months — see seasonal dips and growth patterns.

Example 2: Daily stock price movement of a company over a quarter.

Example 3: Year-over-year revenue comparison for 3 product lines from 2019–2024.

Volume + Trend

2. Area Chart

An area chart is a line chart with the area below the line filled in. It emphasizes the magnitude or volume of a trend, not just its direction. The filled area draws attention to the "weight" of the data.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Showing volume beneath a trend line
  • • Emphasizing total value across time (not just change)
  • • Single series where magnitude matters as much as direction

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Total sales volume over 12 months — the filled area shows the "weight" of sales.

Example 2: Website page views per day — area conveys total traffic volume.

Example 3: Cumulative energy consumption over a year for a manufacturing plant.

Composition + Trend

3. Stacked Area Chart

A stacked area chart shows multiple series stacked on top of each other, revealing both the total and the composition. Each band represents a category, and together they show the whole.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Showing part-to-whole relationships over time
  • • Comparing contribution of multiple categories
  • • Total + breakdown simultaneously

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Revenue by product category (Electronics, Clothing, Food) over 5 years — see each category's contribution.

Example 2: Website traffic sources (Organic, Paid, Social, Direct) by month.

Example 3: Energy production by source (Solar, Wind, Hydro, Nuclear) over a decade.

Comparison

4. Clustered Column Chart

A clustered column chart places bars side by side for each category. It's perfect for comparing discrete categories or groups. Each cluster has multiple bars representing different series.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Comparing values across categories
  • • Side-by-side comparison of 2-4 series
  • • Categories are discrete (not continuous time)

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Quarterly sales comparison across 4 regions (North, South, East, West).

Example 2: Student scores in Math, Science, English across 3 departments.

Example 3: Monthly product returns for 3 different product lines side by side.

Composition

5. Stacked Column Chart

A stacked column chart stacks segments on top of each other within a single bar. It shows both the total and the breakdown by category, making part-to-whole relationships clear.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Showing composition within a total
  • • Comparing totals while seeing breakdowns
  • • 3-5 segments per bar (more gets messy)

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Monthly total expenses broken down by category (Rent, Food, Transport, Utilities).

Example 2: Quarterly revenue by region — total bar height shows total revenue, segments show regional split.

Example 3: Project budget allocation across departments (Design, Dev, QA, Marketing).

Ranking

6. Clustered Bar Chart

A clustered bar chart is the horizontal version of the clustered column chart. Bars extend left to right, making it ideal when category labels are long or when you're ranking items.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Long category names that don't fit on X-axis
  • • Ranking items from highest to lowest
  • • Comparing 2-4 series across many categories

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Employee satisfaction scores across departments (HR, Engineering, Sales, Marketing, Finance).

Example 2: Country-wise GDP comparison for 10 countries with full country names.

Example 3: Product return rates by product name (long labels) for two fiscal years.

Horizontal Composition

7. Stacked Bar Chart

The horizontal sibling of the stacked column. Segments are stacked left to right within each bar. Great for part-to-whole analysis with long labels.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Part-to-whole with long category names
  • • Showing composition across ranked items
  • • Survey response breakdowns (Strongly Agree → Disagree)

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Budget allocation by department — each bar shows sub-categories (Salaries, Tools, Training).

Example 2: Customer sentiment breakdown (Positive, Neutral, Negative) by product line.

Example 3: Market share by company with product segment breakdown.

Dual Axis

8. Combo Chart (Column + Line)

A combo chart combines two chart types — typically columns and a line — on dual axes. It lets you plot two different scales (like revenue in millions and margin as %) on the same visual.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Two metrics with different scales (e.g., $ vs %)
  • • Showing correlation between two measures
  • • Highlighting a target or benchmark against actuals

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Monthly revenue (columns, $ scale) and profit margin (line, % scale) on dual axes.

Example 2: Units sold (columns) vs average selling price (line) by product category.

Example 3: Actual sales (columns) vs target (line) — quickly see which months missed target.

Advanced Dual Axis

9. Stacked Combo Chart

A stacked combo chart combines stacked columns with a line. You get composition detail from the stacked bars plus a trend line on a secondary axis — the best of both worlds.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Composition + trend simultaneously
  • • Showing category breakdown with an overlay metric
  • • When a simple combo isn't enough depth

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Stacked columns = revenue by product line; Line = overall profit margin % trend.

Example 2: Stacked columns = support tickets by priority (Critical, High, Low); Line = average resolution time.

Example 3: Stacked columns = headcount by department; Line = total cost per employee.

Proportion

10. Pie Chart

A pie chart divides a circle into slices, each representing a proportion of the whole. Best used sparingly — for 3-5 categories max. It's the most recognized (and most misused) chart type.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Showing simple proportions (3-5 slices max)
  • • One dominant category to highlight
  • • Audience is non-technical (everyone understands pie)

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Market share of top 5 smartphone brands — instantly see who dominates.

Example 2: Budget allocation: 45% Operations, 25% Marketing, 20% R&D, 10% Admin.

Example 3: Survey results: What's your preferred work mode? (Office, Hybrid, Remote)

Proportion + KPI

11. Donut Chart

A donut chart is a pie chart with a hollow center. The center can display a key metric or KPI. It's visually cleaner than a pie and less cluttered.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Same as pie, but you want to show a KPI in the center
  • • Slightly more modern look than pie
  • • 3-6 categories with a summary stat

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Project completion status — center shows "73% Complete", slices show task stages.

Example 2: Customer segmentation — center shows total customers, slices show segments.

Example 3: Expense categories — center shows total spend, slices show category breakdown.

Process Flow

12. Funnel Chart

A funnel chart shows a sequential process with decreasing values — from the widest stage at top to the narrowest at bottom. It's essential for conversion analysis.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Sequential process with drop-offs
  • • Conversion / pipeline analysis
  • • Identifying bottlenecks in a flow

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Sales pipeline: Leads → Qualified → Proposal → Negotiation → Closed Won.

Example 2: Website funnel: Visits → Signups → Activations → Subscriptions → Upgrades.

Example 3: Recruitment funnel: Applications → Screened → Interviewed → Offered → Hired.

Correlation

13. Scatter Chart

A scatter chart plots individual data points on an X-Y plane to reveal correlation, clusters, and outliers. Each dot is an observation. No connecting lines — just raw distribution.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Finding correlation between two variables
  • • Identifying outliers or clusters
  • • Large datasets with many data points

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Marketing spend (X) vs revenue (Y) — see if higher spend correlates with higher revenue.

Example 2: Employee experience years (X) vs salary (Y) — spot outliers and trends.

Example 3: Product price vs customer rating — find the sweet spot for pricing.

Hierarchical

14. Treemap

A treemap uses nested rectangles to show hierarchical data. The size of each rectangle represents a value, and colors can represent categories or performance. It packs a lot of data into limited space.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Showing hierarchical data (category → sub-category)
  • • Space-efficient — many categories in one visual
  • • Emphasizing relative size differences

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Revenue by product sub-category within product groups (Electronics → Phones, Laptops, Tablets).

Example 2: Population by continent → country — instantly see dominant regions.

Example 3: Stock portfolio allocation by sector → individual stocks.

KPI / Target

15. Gauge Chart

A gauge chart (or radial gauge) shows a single value against a target or range, like a car's speedometer. It provides instant visual feedback on whether a metric is on track, behind, or exceeding targets.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Single KPI against a target
  • • At-a-glance status (Green/Yellow/Red zones)
  • • Executive dashboards for quick reads

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Customer satisfaction score: 78/100 with target at 80 — almost there!

Example 2: Server CPU utilization: currently at 65% — green zone, all good.

Example 3: Sales quota achievement: $850K vs $1M target — 85% reached.

Incremental

16. Waterfall Chart

A waterfall chart shows how an initial value is affected by a series of positive and negative changes. It bridges the gap between start and end values, showing exactly what drove the change.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Explaining how a total was reached (start + gains - losses = end)
  • • Financial analysis (P&L, cash flow)
  • • Showing contributors to variance

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Profit walk: Revenue - COGS - OpEx + Other Income = Net Profit.

Example 2: Headcount changes: Start 500 + Hires 80 - Exits 45 - Transfers 10 = End 525.

Example 3: Budget variance: Planned $1M + Savings $120K - Overruns $80K = Actual $1.04M.

Detail

17. Table Visual

A table visual displays data in rows and columns — just like Excel. It's the most precise visual for showing exact numbers. While charts show the "picture," tables show the "details."

✅ When to Use:

  • • Users need to see exact numbers, not approximations
  • • Detailed lookups (find a specific row)
  • • Supporting data alongside a chart

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Employee master list with Name, Department, Salary, Join Date — for HR lookups.

Example 2: Invoice-level transaction data for audit purposes.

Example 3: Product catalog with SKU, Price, Stock, Category — for operations team.

Multi-Dimensional

18. Matrix Visual

A matrix visual is like a pivot table in Power BI. It shows data across rows AND columns with aggregations at intersections. It supports drill-down, subtotals, and cross-highlighting — the most powerful tabular visual.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Multi-dimensional analysis (rows × columns)
  • • Pivot table style summary with drill-down
  • • Subtotals and grand totals automatically

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Sales by Region (rows) × Quarter (columns) — with drill-down from Region → Country → City.

Example 2: Employee count by Department (rows) × Year (columns) with subtotals per division.

Example 3: Expense categories (rows) × Months (columns) showing budget vs actual with conditional formatting.

Single KPI

19. Card Visual

A card displays a single aggregated value — total revenue, count of orders, average score. It's the simplest visual but the most impactful for headline KPIs on executive dashboards.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Single headline metric (big number)
  • • Top of dashboard for instant context
  • • Paired with other cards for KPI summary row

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Total Revenue: $4.7M — the biggest number on the sales dashboard.

Example 2: Active Users: 12,450 — the key growth metric on a product dashboard.

Example 3: Average Delivery Time: 2.3 days — headline ops metric.

Geospatial

20. Map & Filled Map

Power BI's map visuals plot data on geographic locations. A bubble map shows circles sized by value; a filled map colors regions based on values. Essential for any location-based analysis.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Data has a geographic component (country, state, city)
  • • Regional performance comparison
  • • Spotting geographic patterns or clusters

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Sales by state on a filled map — darker = higher revenue. Instantly see top-performing regions.

Example 2: Store locations as bubble map — bubble size = revenue, color = profit margin.

Example 3: COVID cases by country — filled map with color intensity showing severity.

Rank Change

21. Ribbon Chart

A ribbon chart is a stacked bar chart with connected ribbons between categories across time periods. The ribbons show how category rankings change — when a category moves up or down, the ribbon flows accordingly.

✅ When to Use:

  • • Tracking rank changes over time
  • • Seeing which category overtakes another
  • • More dynamic than a simple stacked bar

💡 Real-World Examples:

Example 1: Browser market share over years — see Chrome overtaking IE and Firefox.

Example 2: Department budget rankings by quarter — when R&D overtakes Marketing in spending.

Example 3: Social media platform popularity — TikTok rising, Facebook declining over 5 years.

FAQ

Common Questions

What's the difference between a clustered and stacked column chart?
Clustered columns place bars side by side for comparison. Stacked columns place segments on top of each other to show composition. Use clustered when comparing totals; use stacked when showing part-to-whole.
When should I avoid a pie chart?
Avoid pie charts when you have more than 5-6 categories, when slices are similar in size (hard to compare), when you need precise comparisons, or when showing changes over time. Use a bar chart instead in those cases.
Table vs Matrix — what's the difference?
A Table has fixed columns (like Excel). A Matrix is a pivot table — it supports rows AND columns with drill-down, subtotals, and cross-highlighting. Use Matrix for multi-dimensional analysis; use Table for simple row-by-row detail.
What chart should I use for time-series data?
Line chart for simple trends. Area chart for volume emphasis. Stacked area for composition over time. Column chart for discrete time periods (quarters, months). Combo chart when adding a second metric on a different scale.
How many charts should I put on one dashboard?
Ideally 4-8 visuals per dashboard page. Start with 2-3 KPI cards at the top, then 3-5 supporting charts below. Too many charts overwhelm; too few leave questions unanswered. Every chart should answer a specific question.
Can I use custom visuals in Power BI?
Yes! Power BI has 2,000+ custom visuals from AppSource — including chord diagrams, sankey charts, small multiples, and more. But start with built-in visuals (they're faster and more reliable) before exploring custom ones.
