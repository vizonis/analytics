The Backstory

Why I Signed Up for a DISC Bootcamp

After spending 14 years in data analytics — building dashboards, designing data pipelines, and leading technical teams of 10–12 people — I figured I had a pretty solid read on who I am as a professional. I'm detail-oriented. I like things accurate. I care about the "why" behind the numbers. That much I knew.

What I didn't fully understand was how my natural working style impacts the people around me — especially when the pressure is on, deadlines are tight, and my team needs me to be more than just technically right.

"Self-awareness isn't a destination — it's a discipline. The DISC bootcamp didn't just give me a label; it gave me a mirror and a map."

That's exactly what the DISC Leadership Bootcamp offered: a structured, evidence-based framework to understand not just what I do, but why I do it, and more importantly, how to adapt when what comes naturally isn't what the situation needs.

The two-day intensive wasn't your typical corporate training. It was deeply personal. You complete a detailed assessment beforehand, and the results map you across four behavioral dimensions. Then the real work begins — understanding what those dimensions mean, where you over-index, where you under-index, and how to flex your style to meet people where they are.

The Framework

So, What Exactly is DISC?

DISC is a behavioral assessment framework that categorizes how people approach tasks, interact with others, and respond to their environment. It doesn't measure intelligence or capability — it measures behavioral tendencies. Think of it as a lens, not a label.

High C — Compliance

My Primary Style

This didn't surprise me at all. In data analytics, precision isn't optional — it's the baseline. My high C shows up as:

  • A deep need for accuracy and evidence before drawing conclusions
  • Maintaining high quality standards even when timelines are aggressive
  • Systematic, logical approach to problem-solving
  • Being conscientious and detail-oriented — catching things others miss
  • Preference for well-defined processes and clear expectations
  • Tendency to over-analyze when I should be deciding
  • Getting defensive when my work is criticized without data

High I — Influence

My Secondary Style

This was the surprise. I expected to be a pure C, but my I score was nearly as high. It explains a lot about how I lead:

  • I genuinely enjoy presenting insights and influencing stakeholders
  • Building networks across departments comes naturally to me
  • I bring optimism and enthusiasm when pitching data-driven strategies
  • I prefer collaborative problem-solving over working in isolation
  • I can talk through a problem before I've fully thought it through
  • Sometimes I oversell an insight because I'm excited about it
  • I struggle with time management when I'm enjoying the conversation
Practical Takeaways

How to Communicate with Each DISC Style

One of the most valuable parts of the bootcamp was learning how to adjust my communication depending on who I'm talking to. Here's what I learned — and what I'm now practicing daily.

D

Communicating with D Style

They are looking for: Results
  • Be brief, direct, and bottom-line oriented
  • Come prepared with evidence and clear outcomes
  • Remove social chatter — get to the point
  • Provide options rather than opinions
  • Talk about how you'll remove their obstacles
I

Communicating with I Style

They are looking for: Action & Relationship
  • Share your experiences and allow them to share theirs
  • Allow them time to talk — don't interrupt
  • Provide a warm and friendly environment
  • Focus on the big picture, not just details
  • Ask for their opinions and ideas
S

Communicating with S Style

They are looking for: Security & Assurance
  • Be patient — draw out their opinions
  • Don't rush them into decisions
  • Provide clear expectations and reassurance
  • Minimize risk and surprise in proposals
  • Show how changes will affect them personally
C

Communicating with C Style

They are looking for: Accuracy & Details
  • Prepare your case in advance — have data ready
  • Be detailed and organized in your presentation
  • Don't rush them — allow time for analysis
  • Provide evidence, not just opinions
  • Acknowledge their need for precision

Beyond Single Styles

The Combination Styles

Most people aren't purely one letter. The DISC wheel shows how combining styles creates nuanced profiles — each with unique strengths and friction points.

DI
The Challenger
Competitive, driving, results-focused with social confidence. Bold decision-makers who rally people toward ambitious goals.
Words that don't work: "Because I said so," micromanaging
IS
The Counselor
Warm, empathetic, and persuasive. High trust level, accommodating, and great at building deep relationships across teams.
Words that don't work: Harsh criticism, cold data-only arguments
SC

Steady, precise, and reliable. Methodical workers who bring consistency and depth. Prefer clear processes and minimal disruption.
Words that don't work: Sudden change, vague directions
CD
The Reformer
Analytical and results-driven. Private, critical thinkers who set high standards for themselves and others. Fact-based and decisive.
Words that don't work: Emotional appeals without evidence
Key Takeaways

What I'm Walking Away With

The DISC bootcamp gave me more than a profile — it gave me a practical toolkit. Here are the insights that are already reshaping how I lead.

Self-Awareness is the Starting Point, Not the Destination

Knowing I'm a CI profile doesn't mean I get to say "that's just how I am." It means I now know exactly when my C tendency to over-analyze is slowing the team down, and when my I tendency to talk through problems is eating into execution time.

Adaptability Beats Authenticity (Most Days)

Yes, be authentic. But authenticity without adaptability is rigidity. When I'm presenting to a D-style VP who wants the bottom line, my CI instinct to walk through the entire methodology is counterproductive. Flex your style — don't abandon it.

My Team Isn't Me — And That's the Point

Not everyone processes information the way I do. Some team members need reassurance and clear expectations (S). Others need autonomy and challenge (D). Learning to read and respond to these differences has already made my 1-on-1s more productive.

The CI Tension is Real — and Valuable

My C wants everything perfect before sharing. My I wants to share everything immediately. This internal tension used to feel like a flaw. Now I see it as a rhythm — use I energy to get things moving, use C discipline to make sure the quality is there.

Communication Isn't About What You Say — It's About What They Hear

I used to think being clear meant being thorough. For a D-style stakeholder, "thorough" feels like noise. The bootcamp taught me to match my delivery to the receiver's style, not my own comfort zone.

Emotional Intelligence is a Skill, Not a Trait

I used to think EQ was something you either had or didn't. The DISC framework showed me it's a practiced skill — the ability to read a room, identify the dominant styles, and adjust your approach in real time. That's learnable. That's empowering.

Closing Thoughts

My Leadership Growth Plan

Here are the specific commitments I'm making based on what DISC revealed about my style.

1

Set "Decision Deadlines" for Myself

My high C will happily analyze forever. Going forward, I'm setting explicit deadlines for analysis phases — even when the data feels incomplete. An 80% confident decision made today beats a 99% confident decision made next week.

2

Lead with the Bottom Line for D-Style Stakeholders

I'm restructuring my presentations to open with the recommendation and impact — then offer the methodology as an appendix. My C brain wants to build the argument step-by-step, but that's about my comfort, not their needs.

3

Protect Time for S-Style Team Members

My high I moves fast and changes direction quickly. For my S-style team members, that's disorienting. I'm committing to giving more advance notice of changes, providing clearer context, and checking in before pivoting.

4

Channel My I Energy into Stakeholder Engagement

Rather than letting my Influence side create unfocused enthusiasm, I'm directing it strategically — building cross-functional relationships, championing my team's work in leadership forums, and using my communication strength to translate data into stories that move decisions.

5

Reframe Criticism as Data

My high C gets defensive when criticized. The reframe: criticism is just feedback data. I don't get defensive when a dashboard shows an unexpected metric — I investigate. Same principle. Apply analytical curiosity to interpersonal feedback.
